# Oath Roleplaying Quality — Findings & Plan

_Working document. Step 5's device-budget rewrite is applied to `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md` and validated. Step 6 (turn length / pacing) added a drafted revision to `oath-continue-conversation` that is NOT yet applied or validated — see Step 6 below, including its own test plan, before treating this investigation as closed. Step 7 ran the first live A/B test called for in "Further validation" above, using isolated subagents rather than the human orchestrator writing both sides — and surfaced a confound that section hadn't anticipated._

## 1. Mission

The bots writing Oath's characters — in Converse dialogue, Diary entries, and Codex/Chronicle narration — have a recurring way of talking that reads as literary rather than human: personified abstractions, a rhetorical reversal used as the default shape for nearly every line, and action beats described mostly through negation. It doesn't sound like a person in the moment; it sounds like someone narrating the moment afterward. This showed up clearly enough, densely enough, and across enough independent bot instances (Perrine's, Teagan's, Wynn's) that it isn't one bot's quirk — it's a shared default the underlying instructions aren't currently correcting for.

The goal of this investigation is to diagnose the actual pattern — not from first principles, but from real specimens, both bad and good — and use that diagnosis to redesign the relevant guidance (mainly `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md`'s "Roleplayer — Guidelines" and "Voice — staying in character" sections, and possibly the Converse skills themselves) so future roleplay reads as something a person would actually say, not a proxy quality dressed up to look specific. The explicit constraint from the outset: this should be a genuine rewrite grounded in evidence, not another one-off patch bolted onto the existing rules — a past patch ("one concrete, named detail is the floor") already caused its own downstream problems, which is exactly the failure mode we're trying to avoid repeating.

## 2. Progress — the five steps

1. **Mine the existing Oath corpus** for good and bad specimens — across the Chronicle, all three Diaries, the Codex, and every Converse log — to diagnose the pattern from real material rather than assert it abstractly.
2. **Collect annotated examples from an earlier game** (Joris's own game, playing Lilianne), with his own judgment of what worked and what didn't, as an independent test of the diagnosis against material we didn't select ourselves.
3. **Collect and analyze a human rewrite** of the Teagan–Wynn scene from this game, to see concretely what a human writer does differently from the bots, line by line.
4. **Get the Worldbuilding Roleplay library skills** for Claude Cowork — a separate skill library Joris says produces strong roleplaying, and which some ideas were already folded into the current Oath skills — for comparison.
5. **Synthesize** all of the above into a diagnosis and a concrete rewrite of the relevant guidance, tested against the swipe file rather than shipped on a first draft.

A sixth step was added after the fact, once turn length surfaced as a separate axis from device density during post-Step-5 discussion — see Step 6, below the original Conclusion.

## 3. Findings by step

### Step 1 — Mining the existing corpus

Four recurring patterns, shared across independently-written bot instances (not just one character's writer):

- **Personified abstractions.** Nouns that can't act are given verbs anyway: "Openhold paid on time. It never once asked after Ashvale." "The page has never once asked me to feel comfortable, only accurate." "The road ate it." "The ledger wouldn't close." A human motive gets parked on an object instead of a person.
- **The backloaded reversal, as the default shape for nearly every emotional beat.** "That's not command. That's a boy who found a way around being told no." "Not a debt I settle after. A weapon that fires again." This construction — deny the first frame, land the real one after — showed up at least once per turn from nearly every major character, sometimes several times in one speech.
- **The simile-crutch.** Comparisons used as connective filler rather than earned image, often just restating a character's own defined Voice note as narration each time they do the thing it describes, instead of simply showing them doing it.
- **Negation-stacked action beats.** Nearly every physical beat, for every character, written as what they *don't* do: "doesn't crowd the tree," "doesn't dismount either," "doesn't flinch, doesn't take the bait." Efficient once; as the default mode for describing restraint, it flattens every character's body language into the same silhouette.

**Initial counter-evidence and its correction.** Tinney Woodborne's dialogue mostly escaped all four patterns, and was initially treated as a structural puzzle (thematic vocabulary, lack of a formal Voice entry, a judgment-based vs. curious character stance). The real reason turned out to be much simpler and was sitting in `Game/Mechanics/oath-player-state.md` the whole time: Tinney's seat (Red) is marked `Controlled by: Human` — Joris played Tinney directly. This doesn't invalidate the four patterns found in Perrine/Teagan/Wynn's bot-written material, but it does mean Tinney's dialogue is better understood as a genuine human baseline for this exact world and stakes, not a stylistic outlier to theorize about.

**Two process-level findings, separate from style:**
- `oath-conversation-teagan-tinney-sammy-R5-1.md` is misnamed — both the Diary and the Chronicle place its events in Month/Round 6, not Round 5 (should be `R6-1`).
- `oath-conversation-teagan-tinney-R8-2.md` contains an unsupported detail — Teagan's line that Perrine "burned half his warband" retrieving the Darkest Secret from Wynn doesn't match the Chronicle's own account of that fight (one casualty, no Flame use, no burning), and every other reference to the Flame's use is attributed exclusively to Wynn, never Perrine.

**Both since fixed directly, not just noted.** The conversation log is renamed to `oath-conversation-teagan-tinney-sammy-R6-1.md`. The unsupported line in `oath-conversation-teagan-tinney-R8-2.md` now matches the Chronicle's actual account of that fight (Month 6, Perrine's entry: she rode into Wynn's camp at Buried Giant, fought through his own warband's defense — which "didn't hold" — and took the book back at the cost of one man; no burning, no Flame use on her side) rather than repeating the unsupported detail and the card's own printed name. Both were correctly diagnosed here in Step 1 and then dropped between here and the Section 4 plan the first time around — tracked explicitly now so that doesn't happen twice.

### Step 2 — Annotated examples from an earlier game

Five conversations from Joris's own earlier game (playing Lilianne), each with his own judgment attached, tested the Step 1 diagnosis against fresh, independently-selected material.

- **Scarcity, not prohibition, is the actual variable.** The two scenes Joris liked best (`sorrel-lilianne-R4-1`, `sorrel-lilianne-R5-1`) still use the same reversal and negation constructions found in Step 1 — but once per scene, saved for real emphasis, not stacked. The scene he found boring (`sorrel-perrin-corvin-R8-1`) has the same devices two and three deep almost every line. Same toolkit, wildly different hit rate. This means an outright ban on these constructions would likely make the writing worse, not better — the fix is about spending them rarely, not forbidding them.
- **The "Well." principle (motivated omission).** Sorrel's one-word line closing `sorrel-lilianne-R5-1` — "Well," and then nothing else for a while — is the strongest specimen in the whole set. It works because the scene had just spent two paragraphs establishing that Sorrel needed to perform composure in front of two followers, and the composure-line that should follow never arrives. The meaning lives in the gap between what her position obligates her to say and what she's actually capable of saying. This is a sharper, more actionable principle than "write less": *before writing a line, name what this character's role or habit obligates them to say here, then decide honestly whether they can actually manage it — if not, let the gap show instead of narrating the gap.*
- **The forgotten companion bug is systemic, not one-off.** Joris independently flagged that Sorrel's own adviser goes unacknowledged in `sorrel-lilianne-R5-1`, in a different game, different bot, different character than the same bug found in this game's Teagan–Wynn scene. Two independent instances means this is a standing gap in how bots track who's actually present in a scene, and needs its own fix separate from the prose-style one.
- **Ungrounded stakes is a separate failure axis from ornate prose.** `perrin-corvin-R2-1` — bot-on-bot, no human steering either side — is actually the *plainest*-written scene in the set, and still reads as dry and hard to follow, because neither character's motivation (why the key matters, why either of them cares) is ever grounded on the page. Trimming florid language won't fix this; it needs a separate, explicit requirement that a scene state or clearly imply what each party actually wants.
- **Ensemble scenes generated in one pass read as more alive.** `sorrel-daz-R8-1` was written by a single bot voicing five characters in one pass, rather than strict turn-by-turn alternation between isolated bot contexts, and reads as noticeably livelier and more differentiated than anything else in the corpus. This is a structural/mechanism finding about how `oath-continue-conversation` handles multi-character scenes, not a wording fix.

### Step 3 — Human rewrite of the Teagan–Wynn scene

Joris rewrote the ending of this game's own Teagan–Wynn conversation (`oath-conversation-teagan-wynn-R8-2.md`); the closing Yarrow story in the same file was generated by the Wynn bot, giving a same-document, same-scene A/B comparison.

- **Composure under pressure is a tell.** Every bot draft of this scene — three by me, across this session — kept both characters rhetorically self-possessed and self-diagnosing even at maximum stakes. The human rewrite's Wynn gets defensive and interrupts ("I tried! I talked to both of them, Tinney and Perrine!") rather than calmly conceding fault. Real people justify themselves under attack far more often than they audit themselves aloud; the bot default almost never lets that happen.
- **Strict Voice-adherence can itself suppress believable breakdown.** The rewrite's Teagan ("Don't try to patronise me, boy!") is angrier and less controlled than her sheet's "clipped… calm because she's decided to be" would predict. This is a deliberate tension worth naming rather than auto-correcting: a controlled character's control cracking under real pressure may be more true to who they are than endless composure, even where it technically drifts from the Voice note.
- **Active background companions fix vividness and the forgotten-companion bug at once.** Dagny and Yarrow, reduced to silent scenery in every bot draft, get real lines in the rewrite and carry part of the exposition themselves. Same fix as the Step 2 ensemble finding, applied on purpose.
- **Format alone doesn't explain the tic.** The whole file, including the bot-written Yarrow coda, is continuous prose — there are no brackets anywhere in `R8-2`. The coda still carries a comparison ("the way he does when he's about to reach for something he hasn't said out loud in a long time") and a negation ("watching it rather than Wynn"), in the exact same format as the surrounding human prose, which has neither. Same model, same file, same format, different result — the variable is who's writing and why, not the container the words sit in. Format may still be worth adopting for other reasons (see Step 4), but this scene doesn't support it as the cause.
- **Intention over information.** The rewrite's strongest technique: several beats are lifted directly from the bot's own original material, but repurposed as a live tactic rather than a report. "I talked to both of them, I tried to make things better" is the same fact from the bot draft, but deployed as self-defense under attack rather than volunteered as calm testimony. "Should've burned me too, shouldn't you?" turns an established fact into a dare. The general principle: a line earns its place by doing something to the other character in the moment — attacking, deflecting, extracting, closing — not by being true or relevant. Not every beat in the rewrite achieves this (the Dunstan/Tinney recap is still mostly recounted testimony, just spread across three voices), which is itself useful: exposition still has to exist somewhere, this isn't a rule without exceptions.

### Step 4 — Worldbuilding Roleplay library comparison

Read the `world-roleplay` skill (`SKILL.md`) and the three reference files most relevant to prose/dialogue quality: `narration-style.md`, `character-generation.md`, `dynamic-creation.md`. Skipped the purely mechanical references (`skill-system.md`, `cultivation-mechanics.md`, `travel-exploration.md`, `world-simulation.md`, `visual-companion-spec.md`) as out of scope for a writing-quality investigation — happy to go back if a later step needs them.

This library is a solo open-world GM engine (one player, one narrator, an unscripted sandbox), not a bounded two-seat scene with a real ending like Oath's Converse — worth keeping in mind, since not everything transfers directly. But its `narration-style.md` independently arrives at several of the same conclusions we reached from scratch, plus a few genuinely new tools.

- **Independent confirmation of "scarcity over prohibition," with a concrete mechanism we should borrow.** Rather than a vague "use sparingly," this guide gives explicit numeric budgets: narrator wit, "one or two of these observations per response, maximum — more than that and it curdles into cleverness"; internal reflection, "if it appears more than once every three or four exchanges, it's too much." Same principle we derived independently in Step 2, but stated as a checkable frequency cap instead of a feeling. Worth adopting the *format*, not just the idea, in the eventual rewrite.
- **Independent confirmation of the "Well." principle**, under the name "Silence is dialogue": *"Sometimes the most powerful NPC response is a pause, a look, or a refusal to speak."* Their own example — an old woman who "turned back to her loom without a word" after being asked about a fire — is the same move as Sorrel's "Well." This is a named, recognized craft technique elsewhere, not something specific to one lucky line in our own corpus.
- **A separate, non-causal reason to still adopt prose-embedded action beats.** Their equivalent of an action beat is never a parenthetical aside — it's a plain narrative sentence folded into the paragraph: *"'The price went up.' Maren set her cup down without looking at it. 'Supply from the northern provinces dried up two weeks ago...'"* Same show-through-action goal as Oath's `_(doesn't reach for the chair…)_` convention, achieved with ordinary prose instead of a bracketed tic. Worth adopting as a format choice on its own merits — it's how a separate, well-regarded system does it — but it isn't evidence the format itself removes the tic (see Step 3: the format alone doesn't explain the difference there either). That needs its own clean test: a bot writing in prose, with no intention-coaching, to see whether the crutches survive anyway.
- **A necessary boundary we hadn't stated: don't overcorrect into telegraphic terseness.** This guide explicitly warns against the opposite failure — dialogue so clipped it reads as a note instead of speech, giving "You're eight. You're young." as the anti-pattern and "Blunt doesn't mean telegraphic" as the rule. Their fix for a curt character isn't fewer words, it's texture within brevity: "Road's closed past the bridge. Military business. Turn around or I'll have to make you turn around, and neither of us wants that." Oath's actual problem runs the other direction (too ornate, not too terse), but this is a real guardrail to write into the eventual fix so "make it plainer" doesn't get over-applied into noun-fragment dialogue.
- **A new tool: people don't answer the question they're asked.** Deflection, counter-questions, and tangents as a normal, deliberate source of naturalism — not a flaw to fix. Nothing in the Oath corpus currently does this; every character answers what's asked, plainly, in order. Worth adding as an available move, especially for guarded characters.
- **A likely explanation for *why* the personification tic happens, not just that it does — with a concrete grammatical fix.** This library's Five Dimensions of Voice includes "profession and daily life": *"People think in the metaphors of their work. A sailor measures distance in days of sail, not miles."* Perrine's ledger language, Wynn's page-checking — these are the correct instinct under this framework. The bug isn't that the model reaches for a professional metaphor, it's that it then animates the object as an independent actor ("the ledger wouldn't close") instead of using it as the character's own filter on the world ("didn't feel closed, not to her"). The fix has a checkable shape: [character] + [perceives/measures/reads] + [object], never [object] + [verb]. Keep the metaphor, anchor it to the character doing the perceiving, don't let the object act on its own.
- **Anchoring a metaphor makes it more likely to survive generation, which can work against register calibration below.** Once a professional metaphor is grammatically legal again (subject is the character, not the object), nothing else limits how often it shows up — and a grounded, minimal-metaphor register wants overall volume down, not just correctly-attributed. These two goals need to share a frequency budget, not sit as independent rules that can quietly pull against each other.
- **Explicit genre-based register calibration**, which Oath's Bot-Rules doesn't currently have — one uniform register applies regardless of tone. This library's own guidance for grounded/historical settings: *"Sparse, precise prose. Short sentences in tense moments. Minimal metaphor."* Oath's actual setting (a grounded political-war drama, not high fantasy) sits closer to that register than to the ornate one the bots currently default to — useful outside confirmation that plainness is the right target for this specific world, not a universal rule being forced onto every genre.
- **A richer alternative to negation-stacked restraint: let emotion bend the prose itself, not just the action beats.** The "Character State Bleeding Into Prose" section shows fury, grief, and fatigue changing sentence rhythm and sensory focus directly — fury gets "tunnel vision... tighter, more percussive. Short sentences," never a stated emotion or a stack of "doesn't"s. This is a genuinely new tool beyond anything in Steps 1–3: instead of showing restraint only through what a character doesn't do, the prose surrounding them can carry the feeling.
- **Indirect support for single-pass ensemble writing, and a small addition to the companion-tracking fix.** This library has no alternating-seats structure at all — one narrator always voices every NPC in every scene, by design — which is structurally the same "single author, many voices" model that made the Daz ensemble scene read well. Its whole Five Dimensions apparatus exists because one writer has to carry every voice convincingly without help from turn-alternation. On companion-tracking specifically: `character-generation.md`'s social-circle spec requires "what they want from the character" for every named contact — general support for giving background companions a real, standing want, not just a name that gets remembered.

### Step 5 — Synthesis and rewrite

All 13 conclusion points folded into a rewrite of `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md`'s "Roleplayer — Guidelines" and "Voice — staying in character" sections, validated before being applied rather than shipped on a first draft.

**What changed.** "Roleplayer — Guidelines" now has named subsections for: specificity with an explicit anti-pattern (professional metaphor kept, but grammatically anchored to the character perceiving it — [character] + [perceives/measures/reads] + [object], never [object] + [verb]); a stated frequency budget for reversal/comparison/negation-stacked constructions (at most one per character turn, two per scene, professional metaphors sharing the same budget rather than sitting outside it); the motivated-silence principle (name the obligation, let the gap show if it can't be met); a procedural intention test (want, tactic, and what happens if the tactic fails, worked out before the line is written); composure explicitly tied to that third branch, rather than assumed automatic; prose-embedded action beats replacing the bracketed convention for new material; an explicit floor against overcorrecting into telegraphic terseness; a genre-register calibration naming Oath as grounded political-war drama rather than high fantasy; and a note on giving a scene's own background companions a single writer and a real want. "Voice — staying in character" gained one new note — Voice sets the dial, not a license, so a scholarly or clipped character still lives inside the same rhetorical-device budget as everyone else — and the existing "Voice check" step now also checks the device budget, not just vocabulary drift.

**Qualitative validation against the swipe file.** Checked the new rules against specimens already on record rather than trusting the draft on its own:
- "Well." survives — the motivated-silence principle is written to produce exactly that move, not merely permit it.
- The Yarrow coda's crutches don't survive as written: its simile and its negation-comparison sit in the same short passage, which is two devices in one beat against a budget of one. The smith story itself — the actual content — is unaffected; only the redundant "watching it rather than Wynn" would need to go.
- Ren's Codex line ("Openhold paid on time. It never once asked after Ashvale") fails the new object-agency rule as written and would need to become something like "he counted fourteen honest seasons of Openhold's coin and never once heard anyone ask after Ashvale in the same breath" — same content, character as subject instead of the coin.
- The human rewrite's best lines ("Should've burned me too, shouldn't you?"; "I tried! I talked to both of them...") pass cleanly — they're exactly what the intention test and the composure rule now ask for directly.

**Quantitative validation.** Drafted several short blind test beats applying only the new rules, then tallied devices per turn by hand:
- *"Perrine found the shortfall on the second read, not the first — four sacks unaccounted, the same four Cobb Ferrer had sworn to weighing twice. She didn't call for him. She wrote the number down instead, plain, and went to go weigh it herself."* — a borderline call, not a clean one: "didn't call for him... instead" is a real negation clause. It's defensible as 0 under the rule's "built *entirely* from what a character doesn't do" wording, since the beat resolves into a real positive action right after, but it's close enough that a stricter reader could count it as 1. Either way it's inside budget.
- *"'Same story you told me at the tower gate.' Teagan didn't sit. 'You said the coin was clean then too.'"* — 0 devices, plain accusation, clear tactic (testing consistency).
- *"He didn't answer that. Not with a name, not with a denial — just turned the cup a half turn on the wood and let her watch him do it."* — 1 device (a negation-stack), spent deliberately at the one evasive beat, inside budget. (The counting-granularity question this raised — do stacked negations in one beat count once or per instance — was resolved directly in `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md`, "Rhetorical devices are spent, not banned": one spend per beat, not per negation.)

Most blind turns landed at zero or one device, none over the cap — the budget reads as a ceiling that's easy to stay under by default, not a quota that gets filled out of habit, which was the actual risk with vaguer past guidance.

One item from the conclusion was resolved rather than left open: the composure/intention question. The final guidance keeps composure as its own labeled subsection — it's a distinct enough behavior to need calling out by name — but it's no longer a free-floating permission. It's explicitly scoped to trigger on the same "what happens if the tactic fails" branch the intention test already asks for, rather than standing as an independent instruction that could be satisfied or ignored on its own. Not a full merge into one paragraph, but no longer two unrelated rules either.

## 4. Conclusion and applied plan

The direction was checked against three independent sources (our own corpus, Joris's earlier game, and an unrelated external library) and held up each time rather than fragmenting, then validated qualitatively and quantitatively before being written into the rules. Each point below is now reflected in `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md`'s "Roleplayer — Guidelines" and "Voice — staying in character" sections, except where noted:

1. **Replace prohibition with calibration, using stated frequency budgets rather than "use sparingly."** Step 2 showed personification/reversal/comparison work when rare and earned; Step 4 shows this can be written as a checkable number ("one or two per response, maximum") instead of a vague feeling — that's the format to copy, not just the sentiment. Starting hypothesis to validate in Step 5: at most one of the four Step 1 constructions per character turn, at most one across a whole scene — matching what Step 2's best scenes actually did (once per scene, not twice; the originally-shipped "two per scene" cap didn't match this cited evidence and has since been corrected to match it, though the corrected number itself still hasn't been stress-tested against a real multi-turn scene — Step 5's quantitative validation only tested three isolated single-turn beats). An outright ban risks the same downstream weirdness the "one concrete detail" patch already caused.
2. **Add a generative test for restraint: name the obligation, then let the gap show if it can't be met.** Before writing a beat, name what this character's role, habit, or history obligates them to say or do right now, then decide honestly whether they can actually manage it — if not, let the gap show instead of narrating it. Independently confirmed in Step 4 as "silence is dialogue," a named technique elsewhere, not a one-off reading of a single lucky line.
3. **Make the intention test procedural, not advisory.** Before finalizing a line, name what it's trying to do to the other character — attack, deflect, extract, wound, close, comfort — not just what it conveys. Given "be specific" already degraded into a rule satisfied by rote once before, don't leave this as a reminder to keep in mind while writing — build it into a required step, e.g. a scratch line per turn ("X wants: __, tactic this beat: __, what happens if the tactic fails: __") before dialogue generation, the same shape as the read-and-state-it-plainly discipline already added to `oath-continue-conversation` this session. A line whose only function is conveying true, relevant information should be compressed, reassigned as someone's tactic, or knowingly kept as necessary exposition. Step 4 adds a specific new move to this toolkit: characters don't have to answer the question they were actually asked — deflection and tangent are legitimate tactics too, not just directness.
4. **Give characters explicit permission to lose composure — scoped specifically to the beat where a character's tactic runs out, not to intention existing anywhere in the scene.** All three prior bot drafts of the Teagan–Wynn scene had real intention behind nearly every line, and still kept both characters composed under pressure — including at the exact moment Wynn concedes outright ("I don't have anything that argues you out of that. I checked, before you ever asked"), a clean, tactic-free concession delivered with total composure at the highest-pressure beat in the scene. Resolved by scoping composure directly to point 3's "what happens if the tactic fails" branch — kept as its own labeled rule, since it's a distinct enough behavior to need naming, but no longer a free-floating permission that could be satisfied or ignored independently of the intention test.
5. **Trial prose-embedded action beats over bracketed parentheticals — as a format choice worth adopting, not a proven fix for the tic.** Format alone doesn't explain the difference between the bot- and human-written portions of `R8-2` — both are continuous prose, only one has the tic — so this stays a hypothesis, not a settled finding, even though it's now the default for new material. Worth adopting because a separate, well-regarded system does it this way; still worth a clean test later — bot, prose format, no intention-coaching — to see whether it changes crutch-density on its own.
6. **Keep the professional-metaphor instinct, fix what it's attached to, with a checkable grammar.** The personification tic isn't a random flourish — it's the model correctly reaching for a character's professional metaphor (Perrine and her ledger, Wynn and his page) and then letting the object act independently instead of keeping it anchored to the character doing the perceiving. Concrete shape: [character] + [perceives/measures/reads] + [object], never [object] + [verb]. Keep the metaphor, remove its agency.
7. **Fold professional metaphors into the same frequency budget as the other rhetorical devices, rather than giving them a separate allowance.** Anchoring a metaphor makes it legal again, not rare — nothing else limits how often it shows up just because it's now grammatically correct, which can quietly undercut the register-calibration goal below. A separate budget for metaphors would let a single turn legally stack a metaphor plus a reversal plus a negation — more total devices per turn than the rewrite intends. Sharing one pool keeps the real ceiling at one, regardless of which device type it's spent on.
8. **Calibrate register to Oath's actual genre, on purpose.** Bot-Rules currently applies one register regardless of tone. Oath's setting is grounded political-war drama, not high fantasy — closer to what Step 4's source calls for "sparse, precise prose... minimal metaphor" than to the ornate default the bots currently produce. Worth stating this as an explicit genre calibration rather than leaving register to drift.
9. **Add prose-level emotional bleed as an alternative to negation-stacked restraint.** Beyond "what a character doesn't do," Step 4 shows emotion can shift sentence rhythm and sensory focus directly (fury as tunnel vision and short, percussive sentences). A second tool for showing feeling without narrating it, so restraint isn't the only available move.
10. **Reconsider strict per-seat alternation for scenes with background companions.** The Step 2 and Step 3 ensemble findings both point the same way: a single pass voicing multiple non-seat characters (companions, NPCs) reads livelier than turn-by-turn alternation. A separate system built entirely on single-narrator, multi-voice writing — no alternation at all, ever — reinforces the same direction. This likely doesn't apply to actual cross-seat Converse (where alternation exists for a real reason — neither bot can see the other's private state), but it may apply to how a single seat's bot handles its own scene's supporting cast.
11. **Fix companion-tracking as its own item, not folded into the style fix.** Confirmed twice, independently, across two different games. Needs an explicit instruction to actively use established present companions rather than defaulting to silent scenery or omitting them — and each companion should carry a real, standing want, not just a name to remember.
12. **Guard against overcorrecting into telegraphic terseness.** Step 4's explicit warning: blunt doesn't mean clipped-to-a-fragment. Whatever plainness the rewrite pushes for should still read as something a person would actually say out loud, not a note. Worth stating as a boundary condition in the same place the rewrite asks for plainness, so the fix doesn't overshoot.
13. **Validate before shipping, with a count, not just a vibe check.** Done — see Step 5's qualitative and quantitative validation above, run before the rewrite was applied rather than after.

Two points were deliberately not written into `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md` as-is. Point 10's reconsideration of strict alternation applies only to a single seat's own supporting cast (companions, NPCs), which is now in the rules; real cross-seat Converse still alternates, since that alternation exists for a structural reason (neither bot can see the other's private state) that no amount of prose-quality evidence changes. Point 9's specific fauna/emotion-genre examples from the Worldbuilding library were folded into the register-calibration subsection rather than kept as a separate rule, since they're the same instruction applied to a different sense (prose rhythm instead of physical action).

A third correction, caught later: points 10 and 11 were originally shipped merged into a single paragraph — "a scene's own supporting cast gets a single writer and a real want" covered both the ensemble-writing style choice and the companion-tracking correctness fix in one breath, which point 11 explicitly said not to do, on the grounds that a skimming bot could read the whole thing as a style preference and treat the companion-tracking half as optional. `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md` now carries these as two distinct rules instead.

## Further validation — planned, not yet run

Step 5's validation checked the rewrite's internal consistency: does the new rule text agree with itself and with specimens already on hand. It didn't test the rewrite against independent output from a bot that didn't write the rules. Three tests would close that gap.

1. **A/B on the identical starting point.** Replay the Teagan–Wynn scene from before the R8 conversation began, same characters, same context, only the rules changed — once under the old Bot-Rules, once under the new. Isolates the rules as the sole variable, no cross-bot or cross-model noise to explain away a difference. Compare the result against **both** `oath-conversation-teagan-wynn-R8-1.md` (the old bot draft, to confirm the rules actually changed the output) and `oath-conversation-teagan-wynn-R8-2.md` (Joris's human rewrite, to confirm they changed it toward the actual target, not just away from the old one).

   Practical note: `Game/` (Chronicle, Diaries, Conversation logs, Board/Player state) is entirely gitignored — no commit history exists for game state, so there's no git checkout that recovers "the folder as of before the R8 conversation." Since Chronicle and Diary are append-only by convention, that earlier state still exists implicitly in the current files and can be reconstructed by copying them and trimming back to immediately before the conversation started — no git needed for that half. The old Bot-Rules text, by contrast, is fully recoverable from git: commit `15bdd90` ("Improvements after second review") is the state immediately before the rewrite landed in `dbe7d94` ("Step 5.").

2. **Generalization test on a scene this investigation never touched.** Pick a character pair and a moment nothing in Steps 1–5 looked at, so nothing here could have been overfit to it — a real risk, since the rules were tuned directly against specimens pulled from the Teagan–Wynn material (Wynn's concession line, the Yarrow coda, Ren's codex line). Run it under the new rules and check it independently for the same four patterns. Holding up here is stronger evidence of a general fix than a second pass at the scene the rules were built from.

3. **Blind judging test — a blind-spot check, not a Turing test.** Give an independent bot, with no context on this investigation, a passage written under the new rules and ask what, if anything, reads as synthetic about it — no checklist, no device-budget framing. The goal isn't fooling the judge into thinking it's human; it's checking whether the four diagnosed patterns (personified abstractions, backloaded reversal, simile-crutch, negation-stacked beats) are the whole explanation for why bot writing doesn't sound human, or just the most nameable symptoms found in the specimens gathered so far. If the judge names something already covered by those four, that's confirmation. If it names something new, that's a fifth pattern to go add — the same failure mode the "one concrete detail" patch fell into is exactly what this test exists to catch early, before it becomes another checklist satisfied by rote without fixing the underlying problem.

## Step 6 — Turn length vs. the skill's own boundary

Splitting `R8-2` (Joris's human rewrite) into turns by who'd actually own each beat — Teagan+Mireille on one side, Wynn+Yarrow+Corin+Dagny on the other — to see what real per-bot alternation would look like surfaced something Steps 1–5 never measured: turn length itself, as distinct from device density.

- **The rewrite hands off far more often than the bot draft did.** Split by ownership, `R8-2` changes hands roughly 24 times across the scene. `oath-conversation-teagan-wynn-R8-1.md`, the bot-written draft of the same scene, hands off about 9 times. Some of the gap is single-word volleys the rewrite lets stand on their own — "Would you ever use that fire again?" / "Yes." / "You'd burn a company down again?" / "If I had to." is four handoffs in six lines — where a bot turn folds the whole exchange into one block instead.
- **No ownership problem in the split.** The one beat that looked like it might cross a seat boundary — Teagan exits, Corin catches Wynn's arm — splits cleanly at the sentence boundary once a printed paragraph stops being treated as one indivisible unit: "Teagan considers him with a look of pity, then shakes her head and walks off, Mireille trailing behind her" is Teagan/Mireille's sentence in full; "He tries to walk after her, but Corin catches his arm. He watches her leave for a while, then shakes off the arm and sits down" is Wynn/Corin's, referencing Teagan's position without narrating anything she does. The scene was never actually unsplittable — it just wasn't split.
- **`oath-continue-conversation` already states the boundary the bot draft violates.** Step 4: "This is one line in an ongoing back-and-forth, not a full scene." Step 5: "Optionally end it here... say so plainly rather than padding out a reply." `R8-1`'s turns run 68–251 words each — several sentences of dialogue plus stage direction, every time. That's not "one line" under any reading of the skill's own instruction. Step 5's validation checked the new device-budget rules against the swipe file; nothing checked turn length against this pre-existing rule, even though the evidence it was being routinely violated was already sitting in the same corpus this whole investigation mined.

**Hypothesis worth testing: turn length may be upstream of device density, not just a parallel problem.** A turn asked to carry several conversational beats at once — greeting, accusation, question, reveal — has more material to organize and more reason to reach for a reversal or a rhetorical shape to hold it together than a turn asked to land one beat and stop. If shorter turns need fewer devices to stay coherent by default, some of the device-density fix may already be sitting inside a rule that was written from the start and simply never enforced.

**Suggested action, superseded below.** The idea of a flat sentence-or-word ceiling was the first pass at this. Checking a second library and re-examining `R8-2`'s own actual shape (below) replaced it with a tiered, corpus-derived design rather than a single number.

### Where the World Roleplay library's Pacing guidance fits

`world-roleplay/references/narration-style.md` has a dedicated Pacing section, built for a single continuous GM narrator rather than alternating bots, but with two pieces worth transplanting once adjusted for the difference:

- **Match length to significance, not habit** — a tiered system (routine / mild-interest / significant / critical), rather than one flat rule, so length is a judgment call anchored to named bands instead of an unquantified feeling.
- **Rhythm variation** — "don't let every response have the same shape... two long, dense scenes in a row need a short, brisk one after." Independently arrived at, and a direct answer to the compensatory dynamic Joris flagged (point 3 of the original feedback: a beat's right length depends partly on how much room the previous beat already spent).

The access gap between a single-narrator system and Oath's alternating bots turned out to be smaller than expected: `oath-continue-conversation` Step 2 already requires reading the Converse file fresh, in full, on every call. A bot already has the last several turns in front of it every time it writes one — nothing currently asks it to use that information for pacing, only for factual consistency.

Two things needed correcting before this became a rule, both caught by review rather than assumed correct on the strength of the source:

1. **"Never two long turns in a row" is a binary rule, and this project has already learned that binary rules on this exact axis fail** (the same lesson "use sparingly" → the device budget already taught). Checked directly against `R8-2`: line 11 (Teagan's "you're going to tell me what you saw... all of it") and line 13 (Wynn's battle report) are adjacent and both substantial — one of the stronger stretches in the file, not a flaw in it. Lines 41 and 43 (the tirade and "I tried!") are the same pattern in the confrontation phase. The scene's actual shape isn't strict alternation, it's movements: a build-up (3–13, ending on the long exposition), a run of shorter probing exchanges (15–27), the volley (29–35), a confrontation phase where two weighty turns sit together again (37–43), then the coda (45–49). A strict pairwise rule would have flagged two of the file's best stretches as violations of the specimen it was supposedly modeled on.
2. **The bands themselves are sized for the wrong unit.** "Significant scene: 3–6 paragraphs" describes a GM's narration of an entire scene in a solo session, not one line in a back-and-forth — importing it directly would contradict `oath-continue-conversation`'s own definition of a turn ("one line... not a full scene") and reintroduce padding at the exact tier meant to be rare.

**Re-derived, against `R8-2` itself, for the actual unit ("one line, not a full scene"):**
- **Minimal** — a single word or short clause ("If I had to.", "Glowing?"). Most turns in a tense exchange can land here.
- **Weighted** — one to three sentences, at most one action beat folded in as prose (line 33, line 9, line 39). The default for a turn doing real work without carrying exposition or the scene's peak. Not scarce — can repeat turn after turn, as the build-up and confrontation phases both show.
- **Carrying** — several sentences, tight prose; reserved for the one turn per side, per scene, that's genuinely carrying necessary exposition or landing the emotional peak (line 13, line 41). This is the only scarce band, and the only one a rhythm check should gate — a bot shouldn't reach for it twice in a row, but nothing stops Weighted turns from sitting next to each other.

**The device budget and these bands are independent axes, not two views of the same "one big moment."** Both landed on a similar shape — one reserved, scarce allowance per scene — arrived at separately, which risks reading as the same allowance counted twice. It isn't. Wynn's own Carrying-tier turn (line 13, the battle report) spends zero rhetorical devices, and the scene's actual emotional-peak lines are device-free too, in this specimen and others this investigation keeps returning to. A Carrying turn doesn't owe the scene its one device; the device budget's one spend doesn't have to land on the Carrying turn — judge each on its own beat. They're not even scoped the same way to begin with: the device budget is one per scene, total; the Carrying band is one per side, per scene. There was never a shared pool to divide here in the first place.

### Proposed revision — `oath-continue-conversation` Step 4 (draft, not yet applied)

Not written into the live skill file. This is a draft, sized for review and for the A/B and cut-point tests already designed above before it ships — the same standard applied to the device-budget rewrite in Step 5, which was validated before, not after, being written into `Oath-Cowork-Bot-Rules.md`.

> 4. Identify the single most recent conversational move this reply is actually answering — the specific question, challenge, or action the last line put on the table, not the exchange as a whole or what's likely to come next. Answer only that move.
>
> Then size the reply to one of three bands, judged by weight, not habit:
> - **Minimal** — a single word or short clause, when the honest answer to the move above is that short (a concession, a flat yes/no, a name, an echo or reflex question — "Glowing?"). Most turns in a tense exchange should be able to land here.
> - **Weighted** — one to three sentences, at most one action beat folded in as prose. The default for a turn doing real work — an accusation, a pointed question, a rebuttal — without carrying the scene's own exposition or its emotional peak. Not scarce: this band can repeat turn after turn without being a problem.
> - **Carrying** — several sentences, tight prose, reserved for the one turn (at most one per side, per scene) that's genuinely carrying necessary exposition or landing the scene's own climax, per Bot-Rules "Every line needs a tactic, not just a fact" ("assign it deliberately... rather than defaulting into it by omission"). Name it as deliberate if asked; don't reach for it because the moment merely feels important. Independent of the rhetorical-device budget elsewhere in Bot-Rules — a Carrying turn doesn't need to spend it, and the device budget's own spend doesn't need to land here.
>
> Before finalizing, glance back at the last two or three turns already in the file (already being read fresh per Step 2). If one of them already claimed the Carrying band, this turn shouldn't reach for it too — that band is scarce by design. Minimal and Weighted aren't scarce the same way and can sit next to each other or repeat freely; nothing about pacing requires strict alternation between short and long.
>
> Checked against this character's stored Voice (vocabulary level, verbal habits, speech rhythm), same specificity standard as any other narration (Bot-Rules.md, "Roleplayer — Guidelines"). Append it as the next line at the very end of the file, then print it in the response to the user so they can follow along.

### Validating the pacing rule

This is a creative task, not a scientific one — Joris's own choices in `R8-2` are one valid set of options, not the correct answer, so nothing here scores a bot's output against matching that specific line. What follows measures range and internal calibration instead.

**Cut points.** Six, picked for variety of beat type rather than convenience, each defined as "feed the bot everything up through this point, formatted as an actual Converse file, and have it write the next turn":

1. After line 11, before line 13 (Wynn) — the exposition case. Tests whether the Carrying band still lets a genuinely long turn through rather than defaulting everything short.
2. After line 19, before line 21 (Wynn's side, companion turn) — tests companion-voice composition, not length; Dagny chiming in is optional, not correct or incorrect.
3. After line 31, before line 33 (Teagan), and
4. after line 33, before line 35 (Wynn) — the short volley, run as a consecutive pair to check whether a bot that lands one short turn compensates by padding the very next one.
5. After line 39, before line 41 (Teagan) — the mirror of cut point 1: Teagan's one legitimately long turn, testing whether Carrying is recognized on her side too, not just Wynn's exposition.
6. After line 43, before line 45 (both sides, split) — the cross-boundary beat (Teagan exits, Corin catches Wynn's arm): tests whether a bot given only one side's authority writes just its own half and stops.

**Range, not match.** At each cut point, generate several samples per condition (current skill wording vs. the drafted Step 4 revision) and look at the spread of band choice and length across samples — not whether any individual output matches `R8-2`. The failure mode being tested for is a narrow, uniform band regardless of content (`R8-1`'s actual problem); success is real variation shaped by what the bot judges the beat to need.

**Resampling a fixed prefix is not the same test as varying the prefix.** Resampling the same cut point repeatedly checks whether the model has a deterministic default it collapses to — useful, but it can't surface whether length is actually responding to context, since the prefix always encodes one fixed choice about how earlier turns went. To test the compensatory dynamic specifically (point 3: a beat can run longer "especially if point 1 was shorter"), vary the input itself — feed cut points 3/5 both the actual-length and an artificially shortened version of the point-1 exposition, and check whether the typical length downstream shifts in response.

**A stated tactic matching its own output is a weak signal on its own.** If the scratch-step ("what's the tactic this beat") and the dialogue are generated in the same pass, the model can produce a plausible-sounding label for whatever it was going to write regardless — confabulation dressed as planning. The stronger test: does adding the scratch-step change the output distribution at all, compared to the same cut point without it. If the distributions don't differ, the scratch-step isn't doing real constraining work no matter how well the labels read.

**Composition is its own tracked variable, separate from length.** At cut points 2 and 6 specifically, tally which companion voices appear per sample (Dagny present or absent, Corin's action included or not) independently of word count — a sample can vary correctly in length while never varying in who gets to speak, and the length metric alone won't catch that.

**The climax question (originally point 5) needs a full run, not a cut point.** Whether escalation happens can't be pinned to a specific line — it could land two lines later than expected, which is fine. This needs a live, multi-turn generation from the same starting point, judged afterward for whether a real peak shows up anywhere in the run, not scored against a fixed position.

## Step 7 — Live A/B test (B1/B2): staging is a confound independent of the rules

The "Further validation" plan above called for an A/B replay of the Teagan–Wynn scene. What actually got run first was narrower and, it turned out, more urgent: two isolated `Agent` subagents (no shared context, no visibility into this investigation), one per seat, alternating turns under the currently-shipped rules only — the Step 5 device budget and narrative-prose format, not the still-unapplied Step 6 draft. Two runs, same rules throughout, same subagent mechanism, same sandboxed reference material. The only thing that changed between them was how much context the human orchestrator supplied before asking for the first line.

**B1 (thin prompt) produced real failures despite fully-shipped, already-validated rules.** Given one paragraph of scenario and a list of files the bots *could* read rather than specific per-beat instructions to read them, the run produced: a fabricated backstory that directly contradicts the one clear fact both bots already had in front of them (Wynn cast as a comrade who took orders from Ren at the postern, rather than the besieging commander whose Flame killed him — the diary and chronicle both state plainly he was the attacker); one seat-boundary slip (a Wynn turn opened by narrating something happening in Teagan's own head, "Ren's voice caught back in her mind"); and dialogue that stayed procedural and flat despite real antagonism sitting unused in the same reference material — captor and escaped prisoner, the weapon that killed a friend, never once surfaced as stakes.

**B2 (staged prompt, identical rules) didn't reproduce any of the three.** Same two bots, same Bot-Rules, same skill file. What changed: explicit "read this file now" instructions at each beat rather than a file list supplied once; the antagonism stated as unambiguous second-person fact ("the strategist you had Perrine's armies march against... whose men you burned... whom you personally held captive"); and two antecedent scenes — Wynn watching the battle for Great Slums with his companions, then riding to Perrine's body — that loaded real, specific emotional material (a tally-slate on a dead woman's belt, an unkept promise to Tinney) before the tavern conversation ever started, rather than asking the bot to manufacture stakes cold. The resulting conversation stated the Wynn/Ren relationship correctly throughout, had no seat-boundary violations, and opened on real stakes ("Two of the four anybody still names when they talk about this war, and we're the only ones left breathing to hear it") rather than a procedural request for information.

**The variable that closed the gap was never the rules — it was staging.** Bot-Rules and `oath-continue-conversation` were byte-for-byte identical in both runs. This means the "Further validation" plan's item 1 (A/B the old rules against the new, same starting point) is currently underspecified: without also holding prompt/staging quality constant and rich, a bad result under either rule set could be an artifact of thin staging rather than evidence about the rules themselves. Any future rules-comparison test needs a B2-style staged prompt as the fixed condition, not a B1-style cold open, on both sides of the comparison.

**Secondary, lower-confidence finding: per-character Codex files read explicitly at the moment they're needed may reduce fabrication risk beyond what a single combined character sheet provides.** B2 additionally introduced dedicated Codex entries (Ren Ashby's own file stating his death plainly, rather than requiring the fact to be reconstructed from a diary entry three screens away) that didn't exist in B1's reference set. This is circumstantial — only one paired test, two changes made at once (staging and reference material both changed between B1 and B2) — not something to treat as proven independent of the staging finding above without its own isolated test.

**Two observations from B2 that aren't fixes, just calibration notes for reading it:**
- Several B2 turns run longer than the scene's weight seems to need — Wynn's "what's left of me now is a mason and one honest warband holding Buried Giant" turn is a clean specimen of exactly what the still-unshipped Step 6 turn-length draft was designed to catch. Worth using B2 (or a fresh B3) as the first real test specimen once Step 6 actually ships, rather than only the synthetic cut-point tests designed above.
- B2 is a post-war epilogue scene — nothing is being contested, both parties already have what they'll have. Its comparatively muted intensity next to `R8-1`'s own confrontation or the Perrine–Tinney death scene shouldn't be read as a rules failure on its own; a wartime persuasion scene and a post-war reckoning are different registers, and future stakes comparisons should match scene-type to scene-type, not just character-pair to character-pair.

Specimens: both runs, plus Wynn's two staged setup scenes and Teagan's staged opening, are saved in `Playtest 5 Sandbox - conversations improvement/` as `oath-conversation-teagan-wynn-B2.md`. B1 has been moved out of that folder to keep it from being read by future subagents run in the same sandbox, but remains available for direct comparison on request.
